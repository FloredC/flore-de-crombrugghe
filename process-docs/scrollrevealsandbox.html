Scroll Reveal Sandbox

Six experiments, running live on your own type and tokens. Scroll each one into view the way a visitor would, tune the dials until it feels right, then read the numbers off the bottom. Nothing here is in the site yet.

Three decisions, in order

1 · The unit

What counts as one thing arriving — a whole section, the blocks inside it, or the items inside those. This is the choice that decides how the page feels; the rest is tuning.

2 · The gesture

How it moves. Fade alone, fade plus a small rise, or a scale from an anchor point. Your brief — shouldn't move too much — is a number: it's the distance dial, and it lives between 8 and 16px.

3 · The trigger

One-shot on entry, or mapped to scroll position so it has no moment at all. These feel genuinely different, and E5 is the one most people mean by "modern".

The one that's expensive to change later

Your Rega avatar already states the right principle in its own comment: nothing has to run for it to look right. Scroll reveals break that by default — the usual recipe puts opacity:0 in the stylesheet and has JavaScript remove it, so a failed observer, a JS error or an old browser leaves a blank page.

The fix is to invert it: the finished state is the base rule, and JS adds a flag to <html> before first paint that arms the hidden state. This page does exactly that — the attribute is data-rv-armed. Retrofitting it later means touching every reveal, so it's worth settling now.

Homepage and case studies want different grammar

On the homepage a visitor is browsing, and sequence helps — it says what to look at first. In a case study they're reading, and eleven sections that each stage themselves becomes a tic you notice by section four.

So these probably shouldn't share a setting. My recommendation is at the bottom.

E3

The Guide says it

The avatar rises, lifts its arm — the gesture it already owns — and the bubble scales up from its tail a beat later, so it reads as speech rather than as a panel fading in. This is the only lane where the motion is specific to your site instead of borrowable from any other. It's also the one that answers "curiosity" directly: something happened, so you look.

E5

Nothing fires — it's mapped to scroll

Not a trigger but a function: opacity and offset follow how far the block has travelled through the viewport. Scroll up and it goes back. There's no moment to catch, so it never feels like it's performing at you — the page just develops as you move. Scroll slowly here, then quickly, and notice it survives both.

In current browsers this is one line — animation-timeline: view() — with no observer at all.

What this changed about how I work

Everything that moved this project forward came from changing the question rather than the setting. Two passes instead of a better strength value. Subtraction instead of a better prompt.

AI output arrives looking finished, which makes it easy to accept as a given and tune around the edges. The design work is in refusing that.

E6

Over the line, on purpose

42px of travel, 820ms, 190ms apart — ignoring the dials above. It's here so the boundary is visible rather than theoretical: this is the version that reads as a website performing, and it's roughly what most "scroll animation" tutorials hand you.

What you've currently dialled in


    

    
Reduced motion is simulated: every transition is off and each block is shown in its finished state. This is what a visitor with the OS setting on sees — and, because the hidden state is armed by script rather than baked into the stylesheet, it's also what they see if the JavaScript never runs.

  


  
  

    
Where I'd land

    

      

        
Homepage

        
E2 for section blocks, E3 on every Guide. The stagger carries the browsing, and the Guide is where the character lives — you already have five of them plus the hero, so it's a motif rather than a one-off.

      

      

        
Work grid

        
E4 at 40–60ms, first row only. Cards below the fold of the grid arrive with their row, not as one long cascade down the page.

      

      

        
Case studies

        
E5, or E1 with the distance at 8px. Reading wants the page to stay still. The Guide keeps E3 here too — three per page is a punctuation mark, not a tic.

      

      

        
Never

        
The hero and the map. It's the first screen, it already moves, and it's the thing the whole site is about — delaying it costs more than it earns. Same for the nav.

      

      

        
Fire once

        
Reveals don't re-run when a section scrolls back into view. Re-triggering is the single fastest way to make a page feel restless — E5 is the deliberate exception, because it's a mapping and reversing is what it is.

      

    

    

      

        
What it costs to build

        
One hook and one component — a <Reveal> wrapper taking an index for the stagger, plus the data-rv-armed flag set in index.html. Every call site is then one tag, and the dials stay as four custom properties in globals.css so this stays tunable without touching components.

        
The observer work is already familiar here: AvatarPresentingIdle, AvatarRega and EyeAnimation each run their own. Worth folding all of them into the shared hook while you're in there, rather than adding a fourth.
